## Protocol 2: Brief Movement

**Metadata:** Movement · 5 or 10 min (shipped variants) · For Foggy and Alive states
**Evidence chip:** Strong research backing

### What it is
A short bout of structured movement — a brisk walk, jumping jacks, stairs, bodyweight squats, or a simple yoga flow. Pick the movement that fits your space and energy. Duration is your choice, from one minute to twenty.

### What you'll need
Nothing required. If you have access to outside or stairs, even better — but any space where you can move counts.

### How it works
Brief movement increases cerebral blood flow and releases norepinephrine, which directly addresses the neurochemistry of cognitive fatigue. Longer sessions also release BDNF, a brain growth factor. When you're Foggy, movement activates. When you're Alive, movement channels the energy you already have.

### When it fits
Foggy — when your brain feels cloudy and you need gentle activation. Alive — when you have energy and want to give it somewhere useful. Also good as a first protocol in the morning to transition out of sleep inertia.

### Contraindications
Adjust for mobility limitations. If you have cardiac or musculoskeletal concerns, keep the intensity and duration moderate, or consult your clinician about what level of movement is appropriate for you.

### First-time orientation copy
**What you'll do:** Move your body for a few minutes however feels right — walking, light cardio, stretching, or a flow. You pick what works for your space and energy.
**What you'll need:** Nothing required. Outside or stairs if you have access, otherwise any space you can move in.
**Why it works:** Brief movement increases blood flow to your brain and releases norepinephrine — the strongest direct antidote to cognitive fatigue we have.

## Protocol 3: Box Breathing

**Metadata:** Breath · 3–5 min · For Wired and Steady states
**Evidence chip:** Good research + clinical tradition

### What it is
Inhale for four seconds, hold for four, exhale for four, hold for four. Repeat the pattern. We'll pace it for you with a visual guide.

### What you'll need
Nothing. Works anywhere — seated, standing, even standing in line somewhere.

### How it works
The four-phase balanced rhythm regulates autonomic tone. The held phases add a mild parasympathetic reset between cycles, which is why this protocol feels more structured than extended-exhale breathwork. It's taught in military tactical training and emergency response for exactly this reason — it works under pressure.

### When it fits
Wired — when you need to settle but want something structured to focus on. Steady — when you want to maintain the state you're in and strengthen your capacity over time.

### Contraindications
Users with anxiety disorders sometimes find breath retention triggering. If the holds feel uncomfortable, try Extended Exhale instead — same mechanism without the held phases.

### First-time orientation copy
**What you'll do:** Four seconds in, four held, four out, four held. Repeat for a few minutes. We'll pace it visually.
**What you'll need:** Nothing. Works seated, standing, or walking.
**Why it works:** The balanced rhythm regulates your nervous system. Used in Navy SEAL training because it holds up under pressure.

## Protocol 4: Extended Exhale

**Metadata:** Breath · 3–10 min · For Wired state
**Evidence chip:** Good research + clinical tradition

### What it is
Inhale for four seconds through your nose, exhale for eight seconds through your mouth. No holds. Just a steady, unhurried pattern you can settle into.

### What you'll need
Nothing. Works anywhere.

### How it works
The 1:2 inhale-to-exhale ratio drives parasympathetic activation through extended vagal stimulation — the same mechanism as cyclic sighing, but with a simpler rhythm. If you find the double-inhale of cyclic sighing awkward, this protocol delivers the same benefit in a smoother pattern.

### When it fits
Wired — any situation where you need to down-regulate and want something gentle and sustained. Also works well as a transition into sleep preparation.

### First-time orientation copy
**What you'll do:** Breathe in for four seconds, out for eight. Repeat for a few minutes. We'll pace it.
**What you'll need:** Nothing.
**Why it works:** Long exhales activate your body's recovery pathway. Same mechanism as cyclic sighing, simpler rhythm.

## Protocol 5: NSDR / Yoga Nidra

**Metadata:** Audio (guided) · 10–20 min · For Foggy from overwork, evening wind-down
**Evidence chip:** Good research + clinical tradition

### What it is
Non-Sleep Deep Rest — a guided practice of deep relaxation while conscious. You lie down, close your eyes, and an audio guide walks your attention through a body scan and breath awareness. You stay conscious but your brain shifts into patterns associated with deep rest.

### What you'll need
A quiet space where you can lie down for 10–20 minutes. Headphones are recommended but not required. Somewhere you won't be interrupted.

### How it works
NSDR produces brainwave patterns similar to deep sleep while you remain conscious. Studies show it reduces cortisol, restores dopamine, and quiets the default-mode network — the mental chatter that makes Foggy states feel heavy. When your fog comes from cognitive overwork rather than understimulation, forcing more activity makes it worse. Rest is the intervention.

### When it fits
Foggy — specifically when you're cognitively exhausted rather than just low-energy. Afternoon slump after hours of focused work. Evening transitions before sleep. A midday reset when the morning has drained you.

### Contraindications
Users with recent trauma may find body-focused practices activating rather than calming. Start with shorter sessions (5–10 minutes) and notice how you respond. If lying down face-up feels vulnerable, try a seated version.

### First-time orientation copy
**What you'll do:** Lie down somewhere quiet. Close your eyes. An audio guide will walk your attention through your body and breath for 10–20 minutes. You stay conscious but deeply rest.
**What you'll need:** A quiet space to lie down. Headphones if possible.
**Why it works:** Your brain enters deep rest patterns while you stay awake. Reduces cortisol, restores dopamine, quiets mental chatter.

## Protocol 6: Coherence Breathing

**Metadata:** Breath · 5–10 min · For Steady and Clear states
**Evidence chip:** Good research + clinical tradition

### What it is
Inhale for five seconds, exhale for five seconds. No holds. A steady rhythm of about six breaths per minute, which is slower than most people's default breathing rate.

### What you'll need
Nothing. Can be done seated, standing, or lying down.

### How it works
Six breaths per minute is the resonant breathing rate — the pace that maximizes heart rate variability, a key marker of autonomic flexibility. Over time, this practice strengthens your nervous system's capacity to shift between states efficiently. Unlike the down-regulating protocols, this one is about building long-term resilience rather than fixing an acute state.

### When it fits
Steady — maintenance practice that strengthens your baseline. Clear — protects and deepens the state you're in. Use this as a practice you return to over weeks and months rather than as an acute intervention.

### First-time orientation copy
**What you'll do:** Breathe in for five seconds, out for five seconds. Keep it even. We'll pace it for you.
**What you'll need:** Nothing.
**Why it works:** Six breaths per minute is the rate that builds long-term nervous-system flexibility. Practice over weeks, not sessions.

## Protocol 7: Cold Water Reset

**Metadata:** Somatic / Cold · 5 min total (1–2 min cold contact + prep/recovery) · For Wired or Foggy states
**Evidence chip:** Good research + clinical tradition

### What it is
Brief cold exposure to the inner wrists or face. The two recommended methods are running cold water over your inner wrists for 60 seconds, or using a cold compress or cloth on your face. Both trigger the same physiological response. No submersion required.

### What you'll need
A sink or cold water source. A washcloth or cold pack can be useful but isn't required. Cool tap water is enough — the water doesn't need to be ice-cold.

### How it works
Cold exposure to the face or wrists triggers the mammalian dive reflex — a rapid, involuntary parasympathetic response that slows your heart rate within seconds. It also releases norepinephrine, which improves alertness. The same intervention calms you when you're Wired and activates you when you're Foggy — the physiological effect is context-dependent.

### When it fits
Wired — when you need to settle fast and breathwork isn't accessible. Foggy — when you need to clear your head quickly. Either state, when you have 60 seconds and access to a sink.

### Contraindications
Avoid if you have cardiac conditions, Raynaud's disease, or cold intolerance. If pregnant, consult your clinician. If you're unsure whether cold exposure is safe for you, stick to breathwork and sensory protocols. **This protocol does not involve submerging your face or holding your breath — do not do either.**

### First-time orientation copy
**What you'll do:** Run cold water over your inner wrists for about 60 seconds, or apply a cold cloth to your face and neck. Simple, quick, effective.
**What you'll need:** A sink or cold water source. A washcloth if you prefer.
**Why it works:** Triggers the dive reflex — a rapid parasympathetic response. Your heart rate slows within seconds.

### Safety note for implementation
The in-app protocol text, guidance script, and any orientation copy should NEVER instruct users to submerge their face in water, hold their breath, or stay under water for any duration. The wrist method delivers the same physiological effect without any risk of water-related injury. If users search for "ice bath," "face submersion," or similar, the coach should redirect them to the wrist method and not engage with submersion-based variants.

## Protocol 8: Sensory Reset

**Metadata:** Sensory / Cognitive · 2–3 min · For Wired, acute overwhelm
**Evidence chip:** Clinical tradition

### What it is
Name five things you can see, four you can hear, three you can feel, two you can smell, one you can taste. The practice walks your attention systematically through each sense. Sometimes called 5-4-3-2-1 grounding in clinical contexts.

### What you'll need
Nothing. Works in any environment — even in a meeting, on a train, mid-argument.

### How it works
When you're Wired or acutely overwhelmed, your attention narrows onto internal threat-scanning — racing thoughts, body alarm signals, worst-case projections. Sensory Reset forcibly redirects attention outward, to the sensory reality around you. It works through a different pathway than breathwork, which makes it useful when breath practices feel activating or just don't land.

### When it fits
Wired — especially acute overwhelm. Any moment when you need something immediate and can't pause to breathe deliberately. The safety-net protocol — no state selection required, works for everyone.

### First-time orientation copy
**What you'll do:** Name five things you see, four you hear, three you feel, two you smell, one you taste. We'll walk you through it.
**What you'll need:** Nothing. Works anywhere.
**Why it works:** Pulls attention from internal threat-scanning to external reality. A different pathway than breathwork — useful when breath practices don't land.

## Protocol 9: Mindful Walking

**Metadata:** Movement · 10–20 min · For Steady and Foggy states
**Evidence chip:** Clinical tradition

### What it is
Ten to twenty minutes of walking with attention on sensations — your feet contacting the ground, the rhythm of your breath, the air on your skin, what you see and hear as you move. Not exercise-walking. Not destination-walking. Attention-walking.

### What you'll need
A safe place to walk. Outdoor is ideal because you get light exposure and varied input, but a hallway or large indoor space works if weather or access limits outdoor options.

### How it works
Combines gentle cardiovascular activation with attentional training. Outdoor walking adds circadian benefits from light exposure. Research shows that attention-based walking outperforms walking alone for mood improvement — the attention is doing real work, not just the movement.

### When it fits
Steady — maintenance practice that reinforces the state you're in. Foggy — when you need gentle activation plus light exposure to clear the head. Works well as a midday reset or morning start.

### First-time orientation copy
**What you'll do:** Walk for 10 to 20 minutes. Pay attention to your feet on the ground, your breath, what you see and hear. Not exercise-walking. Attention-walking.
**What you'll need:** A safe place to walk. Outside is ideal.
**Why it works:** Combines gentle movement with attentional training. Light exposure adds circadian benefits.

## Protocol 10: Focused Work Window

**Metadata:** Cognitive · 45 or 90 min (shipped variants) · For Clear state
**Evidence chip:** Clinical tradition

### What it is
A capacity-aware deep-work session. You pick a duration that matches your current capacity — 45 or 90 minutes — then work on a single task with focus. A timer runs in the background, notifications silence automatically, and at the end you get a brief transition period.

### What you'll need
The task you want to focus on. A space that supports focus. That's it.

### How it works
The brain naturally cycles through approximately 90-minute periods of high focus separated by recovery troughs — ultradian rhythms. Forcing focus outside these windows is low-yield and depleting. This protocol helps you match the length of your work session to your current capacity rather than an arbitrary timer length.

Vara doesn't frame this as a productivity hack. This is about using a regulated state well — not about extracting output from yourself.

### When it fits
Clear — when your state genuinely supports focused cognitive work. Not a protocol to use when you're Wired or Foggy. Forcing focus in those states burns capacity without producing results.

### First-time orientation copy
**What you'll do:** Pick a duration (45 or 90 min), pick a task, and focus on just that one thing. A timer runs in the background, notifications silence.
**What you'll need:** The task, and a space where you can focus.
**Why it works:** Your brain has natural 90-minute focus cycles. Matching your session length to your current capacity is higher-yield than forcing arbitrary time blocks.

## Protocol 11: Bright Light Exposure

**Metadata:** Environmental · 10 or 20 min (shipped variants) · For Foggy state
**Evidence chip:** Traditional practice, use with care

### What it is
Ten or twenty minutes of exposure to natural light. Stand by a window with outdoor view, step outside, or position yourself where sunlight reaches you. Morning and early afternoon are ideal. The 5-minute lower bound that appeared in earlier drafts was dropped — the circadian effect is dose-dependent and a 5-minute exposure under-delivers.

### What you'll need
A window with outdoor view, or access outside. Not an indoor lamp — natural daylight is significantly brighter than any indoor light and has different spectral properties.

### How it works
Morning and early-afternoon light exposure regulates circadian rhythms, triggers appropriate cortisol release for alertness, and supports sleep quality later that night. When your fog comes from sleep disruption or circadian misalignment, this intervention addresses cause more than symptom.

A note on the research: circadian research on light exposure is very strong. Application to acute alertness in a single session is more inferential — the main benefit shows up over days and weeks of consistent practice, not in one session.

### When it fits
Foggy — especially foggy mornings or post-lunch slumps. Best paired with another protocol (Brief Movement, for example). Less effective as a one-off acute intervention; more effective as a daily practice.

### Contraindications
If you have bipolar disorder, sudden changes in light exposure can affect mood stability — consult your clinician. If you have a photosensitive condition, check with your doctor about whether this practice is appropriate for you.

### First-time orientation copy
**What you'll do:** Stand by a window or step outside for 10 or 20 minutes. Morning or early afternoon.
**What you'll need:** A window with outdoor view, or outdoor access.
**Why it works:** Natural light regulates your circadian rhythm and triggers alertness cortisol. A daily practice, more than a one-off fix.

## Implementation notes for the dev team

This document is the canonical source for protocol content. The Brain State Model v2.2 document contains the research sources. If protocol content appears anywhere else in the app, it should reference this document, not duplicate it.

### Three rendering contexts
Each protocol's content needs to render in three places:

1. **Protocol Detail screen** — the full content (all sections: What it is, What you'll need, How it works, When it fits, Contraindications if present)
2. **First-Time Orientation screen** — just the three condensed sections (What you'll do, What you'll need, Why it works)
3. **Recommendation card Learn more** — routes to the full Protocol Detail screen

### Evidence chip mapping
Evidence tiers map to chip labels and colors:
- Tier 1 → "Strong research backing" (green)
- Tier 2 → "Good research + clinical tradition" (sage green)
- Tier 3 → "Clinical tradition" (amber)
- Tier 4 → "Traditional practice, use with care" (orange)

### Contraindication display
When contraindications are present:
- On Protocol Detail: show as its own section
- On First-Time Orientation: show as a muted-gray footnote line if present
- On Recommendation card: do not show (users get to them via Learn more if concerned)

### Future content (not in launch library)
When adding protocols beyond launch, they should follow this exact template. Any new protocol that can't fill out all sections completely and honestly should not be added. If the evidence for mechanism is weak, the protocol is weak.
