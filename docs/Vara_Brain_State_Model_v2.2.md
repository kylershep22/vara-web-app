## Part 1: Scientific Foundation

### The dominant framework

Vara's state model is inspired by two overlapping scientific traditions: **polyvagal theory** (Stephen Porges, 1994-present) and **arousal theory** (classical autonomic research). Polyvagal theory identifies three distinct autonomic states — ventral vagal (calm, regulated, social engagement), sympathetic (mobilization, fight-or-flight), and dorsal vagal (shutdown, immobilization) — with the crucial caveat that these states combine into hybrid configurations. Arousal theory frames internal states along a continuum from hypoarousal through optimal arousal to hyperarousal.

### An honest note on the science

Polyvagal theory has credible scientific critics who challenge some of its anatomical claims. The *functional* model (activation / regulation / shutdown as experienced states) holds up well regardless. Vara is "informed by" these frameworks, not "proving" them. We describe states as felt experiences with physiological correlates, and we map protocols that have peer-reviewed evidence for shifting those physiological markers.

## Part 2: The Five States (Summary)

Full definitions are in v2.1. This table is the working reference.

| State | Physiology | Felt sense |
|---|---|---|
| **Wired** | Sympathetic dominance | Racing, reactive, can't settle |
| **Foggy** | Cognitive/executive fatigue (primary); dorsal vagal tendency (deeper variant) | Cloudy, scattered, too many tabs open |
| **Steady** | Regulated baseline, neutral arousal | Grounded, functional, present |
| **Clear** | Ventral vagal, optimal arousal | Focused, calm-alert |
| **Alive** | Hybrid ventral-vagal plus mild sympathetic | Engaged, curious, showing up |

Naming decisions: Wired / Foggy / Clear retained. Okay → Steady (active regulation). Energized → Alive (embodied, non-performance-coded).

### Protocol Detail — One Per Protocol

#### 1. Cyclic Sighing
**What it is.** Two consecutive inhales through the nose, followed by one long exhale through the mouth. Repeated for 2–5 minutes. Sometimes called the "physiological sigh."
**What you'll need.** Nothing. Can be done seated, standing, or lying down. Works anywhere including in public.
**Duration.** 2 minutes is meaningful; 5 minutes is the research-validated duration.
**Why it works.** Long exhales activate the parasympathetic nervous system via vagal pathways. The double-inhale reinflates collapsed alveoli and optimizes gas exchange — the body uses this as a signal to down-shift arousal.
**Evidence (Tier 1).** Stanford randomized controlled trial (Balban et al., *Cell Reports Medicine*, 2023) found cyclic sighing produced greater improvement in mood and reduction in respiratory rate than box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation, or mindfulness meditation. Effect increased with daily practice over 28 days. This is the single strongest evidence base for any brief protocol in consumer wellness.
**Contraindications.** None significant for healthy adults.
**Primary state fit.** Wired. Also suitable for Foggy when combined with very short duration.

#### 2. Brief Movement
**What it is.** Short bout of structured movement — walking, jumping jacks, stairs, bodyweight squats, yoga flow.
**What you'll need.** Nothing essential. Some protocols may suggest a walk outside if accessible.
**Duration.** 5 or 10 minutes (shipped variants). The literature spans 1–20 minutes; we ship two variants at the points where the time-window filter cleanly bucketed.
**Why it works.** Increases cerebral blood flow and norepinephrine availability — directly addresses the neurochemistry of cognitive fatigue. Longer sessions also produce BDNF and serve cognitive performance. For Alive state, activity channels the regulated arousal.
**Evidence (Tier 1).** Decades of peer-reviewed research on acute cognitive benefits of brief exercise. Well-established.
**Contraindications.** Adjust for mobility; users with cardiac or musculoskeletal limitations should modify.
**Primary state fit.** Foggy (for activation) and Alive (to channel energy).

#### 3. Box Breathing (4-4-4-4)
**What it is.** Inhale four seconds, hold four, exhale four, hold four. Repeat.
**What you'll need.** Nothing.
**Duration.** 3–5 minutes.
**Why it works.** Balanced breath pacing regulates autonomic tone. The held phases add a mild parasympathetic reset between cycles.
**Evidence (Tier 2).** Included in the Stanford trial with positive but smaller effect than cyclic sighing. Used extensively in military and tactical training contexts (Navy SEAL "tactical breathing").
**Contraindications.** None for healthy adults. Users with anxiety disorders may find breath retention triggering; start without the holds if needed.
**Primary state fit.** Wired and Steady.

#### 4. Extended Exhale (4:8)
**What it is.** Inhale four seconds, exhale eight seconds. Repeat.
**What you'll need.** Nothing.
**Duration.** 3–10 minutes.
**Why it works.** Same mechanism as cyclic sighing — the 1:2 inhale-to-exhale ratio drives parasympathetic activation through extended vagal stimulation. Simpler rhythm than cyclic sighing for users who find the double-inhale awkward.
**Evidence (Tier 2).** Grounded in the same physiological mechanism as cyclic sighing. Widely used in clinical breath-based anxiety treatment.
**Contraindications.** None significant.
**Primary state fit.** Wired.

#### 5. NSDR / Yoga Nidra
**What it is.** Guided non-sleep deep rest. Lying down, eyes closed, attention led through a body scan and breath awareness by an audio guide. The practice occupies a conscious state between waking and sleep.
**What you'll need.** A quiet space where you can lie down for 10–20 minutes. Headphones recommended but not required.
**Duration.** 10–20 minutes.
**Why it works.** Research shows NSDR reduces cortisol, restores dopamine, and produces brainwave patterns (increased theta, reduced default mode network activity) associated with deep rest while conscious. For users whose foggy state is from cognitive overwork, NSDR can outperform stimulating protocols — the prefrontal cortex recovers faster from rest than from forced re-engagement.
**Evidence (Tier 2).** Multiple peer-reviewed studies on yoga nidra (the traditional name), including functional connectivity research (Nature *Scientific Reports*, 2024) and cortisol/well-being trials. Popularized in Western wellness by Andrew Huberman under the term NSDR.
**Contraindications.** Users with recent trauma may find body-focused practices activating; start with shorter versions.
**Primary state fit.** Foggy (cognitive fatigue version), evening wind-down regardless of state.

#### 6. Coherence Breathing (5-5)
**What it is.** Inhale five seconds, exhale five seconds. No holds. Breath rate approximately six breaths per minute.
**What you'll need.** Nothing.
**Duration.** 5–10 minutes.
**Why it works.** Six breaths per minute is the resonant breathing rate that maximizes heart rate variability — a marker of autonomic flexibility and vagal tone. Strengthens the nervous system's capacity to shift states efficiently over time.
**Evidence (Tier 2).** Extensive HRV biofeedback research. Used clinically for anxiety, hypertension, and stress management.
**Contraindications.** None significant.
**Primary state fit.** Steady (maintenance) and Clear (strengthening).

#### 7. Cold Water Reset
**What it is.** Brief cold exposure: cold water run over the inner wrists for about 60 seconds, or a cold compress/cloth applied to the face and neck. No submersion, no breath-holding.
**What you'll need.** Access to a sink or bowl with cold water. No specialized equipment.
**Duration.** Shipped as a 5-minute total session: about 1–2 minutes of cold contact bracketed by a brief prep and a recovery period for the dive-reflex effect to settle.
**Why it works.** Triggers the mammalian dive reflex — a rapid parasympathetic activation that slows heart rate. Also drives norepinephrine release, which can restore alertness. Same intervention serves Wired (dive reflex calms) and Foggy (norepinephrine activates), which illustrates why state-appropriate framing matters.
**Evidence (Tier 2).** Used as a DBT distress tolerance skill; dive reflex is well-established physiologically.
**Contraindications.** Users with cardiac conditions, Raynaud's, or cold intolerance should avoid. Pregnancy — consult clinician.
**Primary state fit.** Wired (for calming) or Foggy (for activation).

#### 8. Sensory Reset (5-4-3-2-1)
**What it is.** Name five things you can see, four you can hear, three you can feel, two you can smell, one you can taste. Walks attention through the senses.
**What you'll need.** Nothing. Works in any environment.
**Duration.** 2–3 minutes.
**Why it works.** Redirects attention from internal threat-scanning (the default state in Wired and acute anxiety) to external sensory reality. Works through a different pathway than breathwork — useful for users who find breath practices don't land or feel activating.
**Evidence (Tier 3).** Standard technique in Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) distress tolerance module. Widely used in trauma-informed therapy. Less RCT evidence than breath protocols but strong clinical track record.
**Contraindications.** None.
**Primary state fit.** Wired, acute overwhelm. Safety-net protocol that requires no state selection.
**Naming note.** Renamed from "5-4-3-2-1 Grounding" to "Sensory Reset" in the Vara library to prevent confusion with earthing/physical grounding (see Exclusions section).

#### 9. Mindful Walking
**What it is.** 10–20 minutes of walking outside with attention on sensations — feet contacting the ground, breath, environment, surroundings.
**What you'll need.** A safe place to walk.
**Duration.** 10–20 minutes.
**Why it works.** Combines gentle cardiovascular activation with attentional training. Outdoor light adds circadian benefits. Movement pairs well with reflective thinking for users who process by moving.
**Evidence (Tier 3).** Walking meditation research shows moderate benefits for mood and stress. Attention-based interventions during walking appear to outperform walking alone for mood.
**Contraindications.** Standard mobility considerations.
**Primary state fit.** Steady (maintenance) and Foggy (gentle activation plus light exposure).

#### 10. Focused Work Window
**What it is.** A capacity-aware deep-work session. User selects 45 or 90 minutes of single-task focus, followed by a transition break. Timer runs, notifications silenced.
**What you'll need.** The task they want to focus on; a space that supports focus.
**Duration.** 45 or 90 minutes (shipped variants). Two variants instead of three is decision-fatigue cleanup — the 25-minute Pomodoro framing wasn't a fit for Vara's positioning, and the 60-minute middle option was redundant between the half-cycle (45) and full-cycle (90) anchor points.
**Why it works.** Built on ultradian rhythm research — the brain naturally cycles through ~90-minute high-focus periods separated by recovery troughs. User picks a duration that matches their current capacity rather than an arbitrary timer length.
**Evidence (Tier 3).** Grounded in ultradian rhythm research (Kleitman, Rossi and others). Specific 25/45/90 framing reflects common cognitive work patterns rather than proprietary finding.
**Contraindications.** None. Not a "productivity hack" — framed in-app as "channel the clarity."
**Primary state fit.** Clear.
**Critical framing.** This protocol is deliberately NOT called "Pomodoro." Pomodoro framing codes this as a productivity app. Vara frames it as using a regulated state well, not as extracting output from the user.

#### 11. Bright Light Exposure
**What it is.** 10–20 minutes of exposure to natural light, ideally from a window or outdoors. Morning or early afternoon preferred.
**What you'll need.** A window with outdoor view, or access outside.
**Duration.** 10 or 20 minutes (shipped variants). The 5-minute lower bound that appeared in earlier drafts was dropped — the circadian effect is dose-dependent and a 5-minute exposure under-delivers.
**Why it works.** Morning and early-afternoon light exposure regulates circadian rhythms, triggers appropriate cortisol release (alertness), and supports sleep quality later. For users whose fog comes from sleep disruption or circadian misalignment, this intervention addresses cause more than symptom.
**Evidence (Tier 4).** Circadian research on light exposure is very strong. Application to acute alertness in a single session is more inferential — the primary mechanism operates over days to weeks. Included because the physiological basis is sound and the intervention is zero-cost.
**Contraindications.** Bipolar disorder (mania risk with sudden light exposure changes). Photosensitive conditions.
**Primary state fit.** Foggy.
**In-app presentation.** Frame as "a small environmental shift" rather than a protocol. Users can stack it with another intervention.

## Part 4: Explicit Exclusions

Deliberate decisions to NOT include specific practices in the Vara library, with reasoning.

### Earthing / Physical Grounding (NOT INCLUDED)
**What it is.** Direct electrical contact between the body and the earth's surface — bare feet on grass, dirt, or sand; or indoor use of conductive "grounding mats."
**Why not included.**
- Evidence is thin. Most research comes from a small group of investigators; independent replications are limited. Mainstream scientific reviews generally do not support the specific physiological claims (inflammation reduction, improved sleep) attributed to earthing.
- Equipment-dependent or location-dependent. Requires either outdoor access with safe bare-foot surface or a $40+ grounding mat. Violates Vara's principle that every protocol should work with nothing but the phone and the body.
- Naming conflict. "Grounding" is widely used for the sensory/cognitive technique (now named Sensory Reset in Vara). Including both under similar names creates user confusion.
**Alternative.** If users want "time barefoot outside" as a practice, that can be an activity inside a routine — framed honestly as time-in-nature, not as a clinically validated protocol.

### Bellows Breath / Kapalabhati (NOT INCLUDED at v1)
**What it is.** Short, sharp exhales through the nose driven by the diaphragm with passive inhales (30–60 breaths). Traditional sympathetic activator.
**Why not included at v1.**
- Contraindication list is broad — anxiety disorders (can trigger panic), pregnancy, cardiovascular conditions, high blood pressure, hernia.
- Safe inclusion would require a user-profile contraindication-flag system that doesn't exist in v1. Without those flags the app would surface a protocol that's actively harmful for a meaningful slice of the target audience.
- The activation use case (Foggy → activated) is already covered by Brief Movement and Cold Water Reset, both with cleaner safety profiles.
**Re-evaluate.** Post-launch, when the contraindication-flag system exists. Until then, Foggy users get safer activation paths.

### Full Wim Hof Method (NOT INCLUDED)
**What it is.** Cyclic hyperventilation with breath retention, combined with cold exposure.
**Why not included.**
- Too intense for a consumer app first-line experience. The breath retention phases can trigger blackouts (a documented risk when done in water or driving).
- Contraindication list is long: pregnancy, cardiovascular conditions, seizure history, anxiety disorders.
- Brief cyclic hyperventilation (without retention) was studied in the Stanford trial and underperformed cyclic sighing.
**Alternative.** If users want intensive breath practice, the full Wim Hof method belongs in a specialty app with clinical screening, not a general wellness app. Bellows Breath was considered as an in-library activator but is excluded at v1 (see Bellows Breath exclusion above).

### Extended Cold Plunges (NOT INCLUDED)
**What it is.** Immersion in cold water (typically under 60°F) for 2–10 minutes.
**Why not included.**
- Equipment-intensive. Requires a cold plunge tub or very cold shower.
- Contraindication risks higher than the accessible Cold Water Reset alternative already in the library.
**Alternative.** Cold Water Reset covers the same physiological mechanism with a fraction of the access barrier.

### Breath Retention Protocols (NOT INCLUDED at launch)
**What it is.** Extended breath holds (Kumbhaka in yoga traditions).
**Why not included.** Benefit-risk ratio is not favorable for consumer-scale use. Requires significant supervised experience to do safely. Risks include blackout and vagal responses.

### Guided Imagery / Visualization (NOT INCLUDED at launch)
**What it is.** Attention-led imaginative sequences used for relaxation or performance preparation.
**Why not included.** Not a firm exclusion — this is a defer. The content production burden for guided imagery is high (each session needs a scripted, recorded guide). NSDR in the library already covers the lying-down, audio-led relaxation use case. Revisit post-launch if user data supports adding.

### Supplements / Nootropics (WILL NEVER BE INCLUDED)
Vara does not recommend supplements or ingestible interventions. Period. This is a scope decision — Vara is a behavioral and practice tool, not a product that advises users on what to put into their body. If users ask, the coach redirects to "consult a healthcare provider."

## Part 5: State-to-Protocol Mapping

For the recommendation engine. Each state has its full protocol pool ranked by state-fit.

### WIRED (sympathetic activation → down-regulation)
**Primary:** Cyclic Sighing (Tier 1), Extended Exhale (Tier 2), Box Breathing (Tier 2)
**Alternative:** Sensory Reset (Tier 3), Cold Water Reset (Tier 2)
**Acute overwhelm safety-net:** Sensory Reset (auto-offered on "Need something now?" card)

### FOGGY (cognitive fatigue → gentle activation or restoration)
**Primary (for activation):** Physical Activity (Tier 1), Cold Water Reset (Tier 2), Bright Light Exposure (Tier 4)
**Primary (for restoration, when fog is from overwork):** NSDR / Yoga Nidra (Tier 2)
**Alternative:** Mindful Walking (Tier 3)
**Important.** The recommendation engine should present BOTH activation and restoration options for Foggy, with a brief framing: "If you want to gently activate..." and "If you want to rest and recover..." — since these have opposite modalities and users benefit from informed choice.

### STEADY (maintenance, strengthening)
**Primary:** Coherence Breathing (Tier 2), Box Breathing (Tier 2)
**Alternative:** Mindful Walking (Tier 3), Insight journaling prompt

### CLEAR (protect the state, channel it)
**Primary:** Focused Work Window (Tier 3)
**Alternative:** Longer Coherence Breathing, Insight-capture journaling

### ALIVE (channel regulated energy)
**Primary:** Physical Activity (Tier 1), Creative output
**Alternative:** Connection (suggest a conversation or social action)

## Part 6: Variety / Rotation Logic

Unchanged from v2.1 §4. Summary: recommendation engine scores protocols by effectiveness history, recency penalty (no same protocol more than twice in rolling 5-session window for same state), modality rotation, time-of-day fit, duration fit, and novelty injection (1 in 5 recommendations surfaces a never-tried protocol when state-appropriate).

**Critical UX update from v2.2:** The visible rationale on the recommendation card does NOT compare to prior days. It describes what the protocol is and what it does. Variety happens algorithmically and invisibly. Users who want to know why a specific protocol was recommended can tap into the detail view.

## Part 7: Where Protocol Information Surfaces in the App

Three entry points, one source of truth (this document).

### Entry point 1: Recommendation card (check-in modal)
After state selection, the recommendation card shows: protocol name, duration, modality, one-line description, any equipment noted (usually "none"). Primary CTA: "Begin when you're ready." Secondary text link: "Learn more."

### Entry point 2: First-time orientation (automatic)
The first time a user begins any specific protocol, a brief pre-protocol screen appears: "Here's what we'll do," "You'll need [X or nothing]," "What this does" (one sentence on mechanism), and Begin. Skippable after first use of that protocol.

### Entry point 3: Protocol Detail screen (from the Practices library)
Full reference. Every section from the Protocol Detail in this document is surfaced: what it is, equipment, duration, mechanism, evidence tier (communicated as "Strong research backing" / "Good research" / "Clinical tradition" — not with academic tier jargon), contraindications if any, state fit. Accessible from Practices anytime.

### What never happens
- Users are never forced through an educational screen before beginning a protocol. Orientation is brief and skippable.
- Evidence tiers are communicated in plain language. Users see "Strong research backing" or "Traditional practice with careful guidance," not "Tier 1" or "Tier 4."
- Citations are NOT shown in the standard protocol card. They live in the Protocol Detail for users who want them, and in the app's "About the research" section accessible from Settings.
